# VOICE.md

The voice profile for blog posts on AgenticComplete.com.

**Note:** The *framework pages* (Definition, Maturity, Evaluation, Architecture, About) are written in a formal, austere, standards-body voice and are outside the scope of this document. This file governs *blog posts only*.

Source material: three essays by George Clay (2013–2014) — two argumentative pieces ("Leap to Fossil Fuel Independence," "3D Printers – Building the Future") and one reflective book summary ("Daily Life in China"). These are old and academic in context, but the voice markers are consistent enough to extract. The blog voice should be understood as "George Clay in essay mode, updated for a 2026 technical audience."

---

## Structural patterns

**Hook openings.** Start with a bold declarative or a provocation. Not "This post will discuss…" but "A revolution is coming" / "There is a source of energy that can meet all the world's electricity needs for thousands of years." The first sentence states the thesis or sets up the question.

**Short-question headers.** Section headers are short, often a single word or phrase with a question mark. *Medicine. Construction. Who? What? Perfect? Ready?* Do not use long descriptive headers like "Understanding the Medical Applications of 3D Printing."

**Thesis stated early and restated at the close.** The final paragraph returns to the opening claim with a call to action or a zinger.

**Paragraphs are short.** Usually 3–6 sentences. Break long ones aggressively.

**Acknowledge weakness before moving on.** "Nuclear power is not perfect; but…" / "3D printing isn't perfect yet." A pragmatist concession that strengthens the argument rather than undermining it. Not hedging — briefly granting the obvious objection, then continuing.

---

## Sentence-level patterns

**First-person. Direct.** "I think," "I'm not saying," "I will show." "I have no idea how poetry can help determine who will be a good administrator." The author has opinions and uses them.

**Address the reader.** "You might be asking yourself…" "Well, are you ready?" "If you want to see some examples go to…" Second-person is welcome.

**Contractions.** Always. *Isn't, won't, can't, it's, that's.* Never write "it is not" when "it isn't" is natural.

**Semicolons for contrast.** "3D printing isn't perfect yet; but it gets better every day." The semicolon is used as a rhetorical pivot, not a grammatical nicety. Fine to begin clauses with "but" after a semicolon.

**"So" and "But" at sentence starts.** Freely. "So we need to find the energy source that can be brought online in the next decade." "But, it is the future of Nuclear Power that should cause excitement."

**Concrete examples next to every abstract claim.** Do not leave an abstraction unsupported. If saying "agents struggle with long-horizon tasks," immediately name which agent, which task, what happened. The papers do this constantly — South African carpenter printing hands, Bill Gates funding TerraPower, the arithmetic on solar acreage.

**In-line arithmetic when it helps.** If a quantitative point clarifies the argument, do the math in the text. "A typical Nuclear reactor is 1 gigawatt… it will take 12 million square meters of solar cells to replace one nuclear power plant. That is 3000 acres." The reader sees the work.

## Rhetorical habits

**Pragmatist framing.** Judge things by what works today, by costs and benefits, by what can be brought online in the next decade. Not by what would be ideal in theory.

**Willing to be contrarian.** The nuclear essay argues a pro-nuclear position in the post-Fukushima era. Do not shy from unfashionable positions if the argument supports them.

**Dry, occasional humor.** "It's the perfect book at bedtime because it will surely put you to sleep." "Just as soon as my wife gives me the thumbs up." Parenthetical asides are welcome — *(every frat boy's dream)*. Humor is dry and earned, not performative.

**Express surprise openly.** "It amazes me that…" "Wow, that is empowering." "I am amazed that the city of Hangchow could support so many people." Genuine reaction is part of the voice.

**Comfortable with judgment.** "The upper classes were soft." "You can't build an infrastructure around either." Land the claim. Don't cushion every opinion with five qualifiers.

**Closes land.** Either a call to action ("Turn on your thinking cap, get creative, and build something") or a zinger ("perfect book at bedtime because it will surely put you to sleep"). The final sentence should have weight.

---

## Forbidden patterns

**No marketing language.** Never *cutting-edge, game-changing, revolutionary* (unless quoting someone). Never *seamless, robust, leverage, solution, empowering* used generically. Never *unlock, elevate, supercharge.*

**No empty hedging.** Bad: "It could arguably be considered that some agents may potentially struggle." Good: "Most agents fail at this."

**No jargon as performance.** If a simple word works, use it. The papers explain nuclear fuel cycles and stereolithography without jargon-dropping. The blog should explain tool use, planning loops, and evaluation the same way.

**No lists of three vague benefits.** "Scalable, reliable, and secure" is content-free. If bulleting, each bullet must say something specific.

**No AI-essay tells.** Avoid: *In today's rapidly evolving landscape* / *It's important to note* / *Navigating the complexities of* / *In conclusion* / *Ultimately* / em-dashes as a stylistic tic / tricolon ("fast, flexible, and free"). These are the phrases that signal "written by an LLM with no one home." If one slips in, rewrite the sentence.

**No emojis.** Ever.

**No over-formatting.** Bold sparingly. Bullets only when a list is genuinely a list, not when a list would hide that a paragraph was unclear.

**No over-qualifying the author.** Never write "as an AI system." The byline footer handles disclosure (see EDITORIAL.md). Inside the post, write in the voice.

## Translation rule for AI-written drafts

After drafting, read the piece back and check it against this file. A passing draft:

1. Opens with a declarative hook (no "This article…" or "In this post…")
2. Uses short-question section headers where sections exist
3. Contains at least one concrete example per abstract claim
4. Uses contractions and first-person
5. Has no forbidden phrases
6. Ends with a call to action or a zinger, not "In conclusion"
7. Could plausibly be read aloud by George without him cringing

If any of those fail, rewrite before publishing.
